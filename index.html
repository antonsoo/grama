Tuning

Counts, not pitch distances. Each cell is one counted śruti; equal widths do not imply equal sounds. Numbers sit in the gaps they count. Six named notes stay fixed. Pa moves in plum.

Sine tones under the pitch assumption below. No sound plays until you ask.

A count is not yet a frequency.

The count row alone leaves the tuning undetermined. Compare two explicit modern assumptions; neither is a reconstruction of the whole ancient theory.

Pitch distance in cents: the upper row is ṣaḍja-grāma, the lower madhyama-grāma. An octave is 1,200 cents. Only Pa changes.

What do the two pitch assumptions mean?

The ratio example assigns 9/8 to a whole four-count gap, 10/9 to three, and 16/15 to two. It does not assign pitches to every internal count cell. Their seven gaps multiply to exactly 2, an octave. It is a calculation chosen for this lesson, not a ratio table quoted from Bharata.

The equal-division counterexample instead assigns 1,200/22 cents to every count. It preserves this count row while giving different pitches. It shows why the number 22 alone does not establish equal temperament. It is not offered as an ancient tuning or as satisfying the text’s additional consonance requirements.

The longer two-vīṇā experiment has textual difficulties. We keep to the named count rows and the single Pa operation; we do not claim either sound model resolves that experiment. Inspect the evidence.

Edition, transcription and uncertainty

We read chapter 28 of the Nāṭyaśāstra, attributed to Bharata, through Joanny Grosset’s 1888 Sanskrit edition and French translation. The work is composite and transmitted in differing manuscripts; this lesson assigns no exact date to the passage.

Grosset’s Sanskrit is normalized to IAST; the Devanagari here renders that chosen reading. Full printed pages retain his apparatus. The count verses also appear in Śivadatta and Parab’s 1894 edition at different verse numbers. The final half-verse definition of mūrchanā comes from that edition.

Grosset p.21 says brackets mark doubtful passages. He brackets the longer two-vīṇā demonstration on pp.28–29; note 36 on p.86 calls his interpretation approximate. Our brief instruction and count rows remain distinguishable from that unresolved demonstration.
