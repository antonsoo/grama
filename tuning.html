    Inspect all note frequencies
    Why can the fourth and fifth stay pure while another gap moves?

    Write the whole-gap sizes as A (four counts), B (three), C (two). Closing the octave requires 3A + 2B + 2C = 1,200. In the initial row, Sa–Ma = A + B + C = 600 − A/2; Sa–Pa = 2A + B + C = 600 + A/2. Fixing these at 4/3 and 3/2 fixes A, but leaves C free while B compensates.

    Pa’s change is A − B. It can vary even while those two initial consonances stay fixed. This is a family inside our chosen whole-gap model, not the complete space of ancient tunings. A successful candidate meets only the conditions shown here.

    The instruction survives.
    Our numbers are extra.

    The count rows and lowering instruction come from the text. Cents, octave closure, equal size for repeated whole-count groups and the optional ratio lock are declared assumptions of this experiment.

    What would this experiment fail to establish?

    A candidate with ordered gaps and a lower Pa is a numerical witness for this count-row exercise. That does not establish a historically used tuning, an equal division of every śruti, or satisfaction of the text’s additional consonance and two-vīṇā conditions.

    Grosset brackets the longer two-vīṇā passage on pp.28–29, and his note 36 on p.86 calls the interpretation approximate. This experiment leaves that problem open. The optional pure fourth/fifth lock supplies two modern numerical tests; it does not replace a study of all the ancient interval relations.
